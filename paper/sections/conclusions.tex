\section{Conclusion}

With an ensemble for Gaean exoplanets and constructed worlds, a modeller could follow the consequences of a world's gravity, size, day and starlight from its global climate into its storms and seas. The adaptations begun
for Terluna's Open Moon are a start toward that ensemble: climate, convection and wave models
connected through their forcing, with custom tools for the radiation and geometry that change between worlds.

ExoPlaSim's developers had already extended PlaSim to other planets, retaining the speed with which a
modeller can explore possible climates. Connecting that global calculation to CM1 and the wave models
makes selected climates accessible at the scales of a storm or a shore. The GCM can then be used to explore a
range of illumination or rotation, with resolved calculations concentrated on states where cloud, rain or
surface conditions change. Because the global and resolved calculations are chained through their forcing, a change in a planet's climate could be followed down to the water arriving in a basin and the waves arriving at its coast, through the circulation that
determines both.

For those calculations to describe the same world, its physical parameters have to be used consistently
across the models. The gravity in a dynamical core also determines the weight of cloud water, the fall
of rain and the frequency scale of wind-generated waves; as the day lengthens, the conversion from rain
to convective cover has to count rain over its intended physical time interval. Where the global and the resolved calculations use one physics package, as in the LMD's models, its parameters are shared by construction
\autocite{spiga2009new}; in codes built separately, as here, they are made consistent by repair. The repairs make
several of these dependencies explicit, so a modeller can vary a planetary property through the relevant processes together. Where a
coefficient instead describes an empirical fit, the work required is different: changing gravity in the
fall-speed law leaves the drop-size distribution to be determined, just as correcting the units of
convective cover leaves its relation to rain to be calibrated.

With the ensemble's reference and calibration tools, that distinction becomes a procedure for specifying
another world: its radius, atmospheric column and starlight determine a new curved-air table, while its spectrum and
columns determine the targets for the radiation fits. The existing offline calculations can recompute these
quantities and compare the atmospheric models on the same layers. Agreement in total outgoing radiation
can coexist with different cooling profiles, so matching the layers matters before a GCM climate is used
to force resolved convection. Otherwise, in the comparison, the response of the convection schemes is confounded with a change in where the air gains and loses heat.

A reduced radiation scheme built from the reference spectroscopy would replace the inherited broadband fits and their successive adjustments. Free tools for the reduction and its check exist, built around
Earth's columns \autocite{hogan2022tool,hogan2020evaluating}, so the reference model would supply them with
absorption spectra computed on other worlds' columns. With the spectroscopy checked against an independent code
and its reduction checked by layer, the two atmospheric models could compute heating over the tested
gases, pressures and temperatures under the starlight for which the scheme was built. The spectral calculation would then take a different stellar spectrum as an input, with the spectrum's effects followed into circulation and cloud. A modeller using the reduced scheme could still explore climates quickly, with the radiation computed from a physical reference that can be recomputed for the worlds being explored. CM1 and the wave models take only the GCM's fields, so a GCM that computes its radiation from each atmosphere's gases, such as ROCKE-3D with SOCRATES or the LMD generic model \autocite{tsigaridis2025rocke3d,wordsworth2011gliese}, could force them as well.

Calibrating cloud requires a further exchange between the models. CM1 already receives the GCM's circulation, with
custom forcing for the transport that its rings and boxes omit, while its boundary layer and convection
develop freely. Once their radiative heating is consistent, the resolved cloud could become the target
for the GCM's cloud amount and water profile at the chosen gravity and day, much as
\textcite{battalio2022interaction} tuned a Titan GCM's convection with resolved storms
\autocite{rafkin2022interaction} and observations. The revised cloud would alter
the global heating and circulation, requiring another resolved calculation in that changed environment.
Iterating between the two would make the cloud calibration depend on weather simulated for the world
itself, with CM1's empirical microphysics still limiting the cloud target.

Those limits also define physical questions worth pursuing across worlds. Lower gravity stretches
dry convection in height and time, while droplets, rain and graupel change their fall speeds by different
powers. The resulting storm has different ratios of ascent, sedimentation and evaporation times, so the
rain reaching its ground can depart from a geometrically stretched terrestrial storm. The scaled wave tests are a complementary case, in which dimensionless sea states agree closely along the tested paths,
with Earth's empirical coefficients retained. Since the ensemble computes both, a modeller can examine which properties of an environment follow a similarity law and which emerge from competing processes whose
relative rates change.

These comparisons can be made on constructed worlds over combinations of parameters that atmospheric escape
would exclude from a natural temperate planet. On a small body with a massive atmosphere, the consequences of low gravity can be separated from those of thin air, and varying the atmosphere's pressure then changes the column mass while
leaving gravity fixed. Alongside planets with different rotation rates or stellar spectra, like those the
THAI and SAMOSA intercomparisons define \autocite{sergeev2022thai,haqqmisra2022samosa}, such cases would test the
ensemble along distinct physical directions. The Open Moon is the first of them,
and further cases would measure the general repairs' performance and determine where new calibrations,
deeper atmospheric dynamics or additional physics become necessary. With radiation and cloud microphysics
developed for other gases and condensables, the
couplings and general repairs could also form the basis for extending the ensemble beyond Gaean worlds.

A modeller can also choose those cases to direct terrestrial experiments. In the simulated storms, for
instance, the electrification graft computes charge separation in ice-particle collisions, where alternative
laboratory fits can predict different charge arrangements. Comparing runs under each law locates the
collision speeds, water contents and temperatures at which further measurements would discriminate between the
laws. A storm simulated in its planetary setting can thus be combined with experiments on the particles composing it,
and the revised particle physics tested in the climates where its consequences are largest. Comparing worlds, modellers could select the experiments as well as use their results.

For a natural planet, the models so developed would refine the climate predicted under its prescribed starlight. For a constructed world, the illumination chosen to sustain a desired climate could change with them, as the dimming of the lunar design changed with the radiation corrections. Holding a target
temperature while adjusting that illumination can change the circulation, cloud and rain, so the storm and sea calculations have to be rerun from the revised climate. Designs with similar mean warmth could
then be compared through the rain on their land and the winds over their seas.
The same ensemble could be used to interpret a world found around another star and to design an environment whose boundary conditions people could choose.

Developed in this way, the ensemble could be run to explore where those environments admit persistent
surface water and what weather accompanies it. Simulated rain would constrain the water budgets of
prescribed lakes; the winds transporting moisture would force the sea, and its spectra would determine
the waves reaching a particular shore. Mixing the lakes and seas, the same winds and the waves they raise would
drive the exchange of oxygen between the waters and the air. By comparing changes in illumination or surface-water distribution through these connected calculations, a designer could turn a broad temperature requirement into a geographically
specific design. On an exoplanet, the corresponding calculations would describe possible environments
within the range of planetary properties consistent with observations.

Across that range, familiar air and water could form landscapes with different rhythms. A slowly
moving Sun could sustain weather through days lasting weeks, while a smaller world's deep atmosphere
could contain towering storms and rain descending slowly toward its basins. Under another star, a
different distribution of absorbed light could reorganise the clouds and the places replenished by
rain. To explore such worlds through their weather and their shores is to imagine places in physical
detail: the passage of a storm over a lake, the return of sunlight to wet ground, the arrival of swell
along a coast. Among those possible environments may be places where life has already developed, and
others that people could one day construct and inhabit.
