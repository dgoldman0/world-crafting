\section{Worlds beyond Earth's range and the test world}

Four ratios between a world and Earth determine where many of the models' Earth values err
(Table~\ref{tab:regime}). Two govern the radiation's paths: the mass of air over each square metre fixes the
absorber paths, which PlaSim's broadband fits took from Earth's column, and the pressures, which RRTMG's
tables cover to 1,053.63~hPa; the scale height over the radius measures how far the sunlit and radiating air
departs from a flat column. Two set the scales of height and time: gravity sets the height of each pressure
level and, with drag and the air's density, each particle's fall speed, and the solar day fixes whatever a
formula counts per day. Along these four ratios, natural and constructed worlds lie off Earth's values in
different directions (Figure~\ref{fig:regimes}). Titan departs along all four, with ten times Earth's air over
each square metre, a gravity below the Moon's and a 16-day solar day, and Venus along the column and the day, with
a hundred times Earth's air and a 117-day solar day; present Mars departs mainly in its thin column, and
illustrative planets with Earth's gravity, radius and atmospheric state depart along the day alone. Among these
worlds the Open Moon alone departs along all four in air close to Earth's, which makes it a test of the Earth
values that depend on them.\claim{W02}

The models take the test world's scenario as fixed conditions. The TSIS-1 solar spectrum
\autocite{coddington2021tsis}, passed through a titania shield in space, brings 1,235~W\,m$^{-2}$ to the top of
the air, dimmed evenly at every wavelength to 1,198~W\,m$^{-2}$ in the design case, a scale of 0.970 chosen so
that the GCM's global-mean surface temperature settles at the design value of 295~K, and since the scale was set
once, for the design case, the sensitivity runs reported below hold their sunlight fixed. The shield removes
the ultraviolet that would make ozone, so the air holds none.\claim{S07}\claim{G04} Beneath the shield the air
holds 1,216~hPa at the water level, with oxygen at Earth's partial pressure of 212~hPa (17.5\% of the air),
argon in Earth's ratio to nitrogen, 400~ppm of carbon dioxide and nitrogen for the balance; land standing above
the water level lowers the global-mean surface pressure to 1.16~atm, and across the GCM's cells the surface
pressure runs from 1.02 to 1.22~atm.\claim{S05} At the ground, seas fill the lunar basins to 28\% of the
surface and rain-fed lakes stand at their own levels, so that 759 of the GCM's 2,048 cells, 40\% of its area,
hold water.\claim{S06} As modelled, the seas are a 50~m slab with sea ice, and the land has a placeholder albedo
of 0.2.

In this scenario's air, every absorber path is longer than its counterpart on Earth, and every layer the models
treat as flat is curved. With 7.25 times Earth's mass over each square metre, the design case's GCM column holds
170~kg\,m$^{-2}$ of water vapour, and 348~kg\,m$^{-2}$ in its warmest test, where Earth's holds about 25
\autocite{trenberth2005trends}.\claim{S08} The curvature matters because the air is tall: in the column on which
the curved geometry was computed, the scale height is about 52~km over a radius of 1,737~km, while Titan's air, at
0.8\% of its radius, is about a quarter as tall in proportion. The air's mass lies on average 48.6~km above the
ground, where a shell has 5.7\% more area than the ground beneath it, and straight rays through that column give
horizon air masses of 7.1 for well-mixed air and 13.5 for water vapour, against the 35 of PlaSim's formula,
written for Earth.\claim{S09}\claim{W02} Because the air is so tall, sunlight reaches far past each column's
horizon: a full Earth day after sunset, about half the air above a place still lies above the Moon's geometric
shadow, before refraction adds more.\claim{S04} Across that height gravity also falls, 9\% by the 86~km
tropopause, so a column with gravity falling as $GM/r^2$ holds about 5\% more air than the $p_s/g_s$ the codes
assume, and spherical shells with that gravity raise the excess to 11.5\% per square metre of ground: for the
same surface pressure, the lunar air holds about a tenth more mass than the models count.\claim{S10}

Gravity's surface value matters more. A sixth of Earth's gravity raises every pressure level sixfold in height,
so a code that sets a feature's height in metres puts it at a sixth of its lunar height. Fall speeds respond as
well, though unevenly, because they follow gravity to powers set by each particle's fall-speed law, slowing
droplets sixfold, rain about threefold and graupel 2.3-fold, the dependence on gravity that
\textcite{loftus2021physics} trace for raindrops across planetary atmospheres. At the sea the same weak gravity
roughens the surface and slows the waves: Charnock's roughness grows sixfold for the same friction velocity
\autocite{charnock1955wind}, and a deep-water wave of a given length travels at 0.41 of its speed on
Earth.\claim{S11} The dry dynamics, for their part, stretch about sixfold in size and duration at the same speeds,
so a 6~km column of a cloud-resolving model stands for about 1~km at Earth's scale, while the microphysics and the
radiation keep their own scales. How far lunar convection departs from Earth's, stretched sixfold, however, remains
unmeasured, so the 6~km grid is justified by the dry similarity alone.\claim{S12}

The solar day sets the scale of time as gravity sets that of height. At 29.53 days it deepens the soil's thermal
wave to about 0.6~m, $\sqrt{29.53}\approx5.4$ times the depth of Earth's diurnal wave, and for the same reason a
formula written per Earth day but applied per lunar day takes nearly thirty times its intended input. The rotation
is nearly as slow: at 27.32 days it sets a Coriolis parameter of \SI{5.2e-6}{s^{-1}} at 80\textdegree, the
latitude of the polar CM1 rings, an inertial period of 14 Earth days.\claim{S13} The tilt, by contrast, is small:
at 1.54\textdegree\ it keeps the Sun grazing at the poles all year, where ExoPlaSim's default orbit puts Earth's
tilt of 1~AD, 23.7\textdegree.\claim{H24}
